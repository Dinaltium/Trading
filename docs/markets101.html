01What the stock market actually is

A company like Apple splits itself into millions of tiny pieces called shares. Own one share, you own a tiny sliver of the company. The stock market is just a giant, continuous auction where people buy and sell those slivers — the "price" of a stock at any moment is simply the last price a buyer and seller agreed on.

🏠

Think of it like a neighborhood where houses trade constantly. If ten people want to buy a house on your street and only two are selling, the price goes up. That's it — that's the entire market, at massive scale, happening every second the exchange is open.

SPY and QQQ, the two symbols this project trades, aren't single companies — they're ETFs (exchange-traded funds): baskets holding hundreds of stocks at once. SPY tracks the S&P 500 (500 large US companies). QQQ tracks the Nasdaq-100 (100 large tech-heavy companies). Buying SPY is like buying a tiny slice of the entire US economy in one trade.

Why that matters for this project: a single company can report bad earnings and crash 20% overnight for reasons that have nothing to do with the broader market. An ETF that holds 500 companies almost never does — one bad company barely moves it. That's why our whole strategy is built around ETFs, not individual stocks.

02What an option actually is

A stock option is a contract that gives you the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell 100 shares of a stock at a fixed price, by a fixed date. You pay a small amount up front — the premium — for that right.

🎟️

Imagine paying $20 for the right to buy a concert ticket at today's $100 price, any time in the next month — even if the ticket's price triples to $300 by then. That $20 is your premium. If the price never goes up, you just don't use the right, and you're out the $20. That's a call option.

There are exactly two kinds:

  • Call option — the right to buy at a fixed price. You buy calls when you think the price is going up.
  • Put option — the right to sell at a fixed price. You buy puts when you think the price is going down.

Every option has three numbers that define it:

  • Strike price — the fixed price you can buy/sell at
  • Expiration date — the last day the right exists
  • Premium — what you pay (or receive, if you're the seller) for the contract
A single option contract on Alpaca (and everywhere else in the US) always covers 100 shares. A premium quoted as "$2.50" actually costs $250 per contract.

03Why we never buy a "naked" option

Buying a single call or put outright is simple but has a scary property: if you're wrong, you can lose 100% of what you paid. Selling one outright is worse — a "naked" short call has, in theory, unlimited loss, since a stock price can keep climbing forever.

A spread fixes this: you buy one option and sell a related one at the same time. The one you sell brings in money that offsets what you paid, and it also puts a hard ceiling on how much you can lose. Every trade this system makes is a spread — never a single naked option.

This is what "defined-risk" means throughout the codebase — before a trade is even placed, the maximum possible loss is a known, fixed dollar amount. That's not a policy, it's a mathematical property of trading two legs together instead of one.

04The three trades this system can make

All three examples below use round, illustrative numbers on a $100 stock — not real current prices, just clean numbers to show the shape of each trade.

Bull call spread — used when the signal says "up"

Buy a call near today's price, sell a call further above it. You profit if the stock goes up, capped either direction.

Payoff at expiration — buy $100 call for $3, sell $110 call for $1 (net cost $2 = $200/contract)
Profit/loss at expiration

Bear put spread — used when the signal says "down"

The mirror image: buy a put near today's price, sell a put further below it. Profits if the stock falls, same capped shape flipped.

Payoff at expiration — buy $100 put for $3, sell $90 put for $1 (net cost $2 = $200/contract)
Profit/loss at expiration

Iron condor — used when volatility is high and direction is unclear

Four legs at once: sell a put and a call close to the current price (collecting premium from both), buy a further-out put and call as protection. Profits if the stock stays roughly where it is.

Payoff at expiration — sell $95 put / $105 call, buy $90 put / $110 call, net credit $1.50 ($150/contract)

05The vocabulary the code actually uses

Implied Volatility (IV)

The market's own guess, baked into an option's price, of how much the stock will move before expiration. High IV = options are expensive because the market expects a big move. It's not a prediction of direction, just of size of movement.

IV Rank

Where today's IV sits compared to its own recent history, on a 0–100 scale. IV Rank of 80 means "options are more expensive right now than 80% of recent days" — a signal that selling premium (iron condors) might be favorable.

VRP (Volatility Risk Premium)

Implied volatility minus how much the stock has actually been moving lately (realized volatility). Usually positive — the market tends to slightly overprice future movement, which is the structural edge premium-sellers are betting on.

Delta

Roughly, the probability an option finishes "in the money" (profitable) at expiration, expressed as a number between 0 and 1. A 0.20 delta call has roughly a 20% chance of being worth anything at expiry. The system picks strikes by delta instead of guessing round numbers — a 0.20 delta strike means something consistent regardless of what the stock's actual price is that day.

P(Up)

Not a market term — this is our own model's output. A number from 0 to 1: the LightGBM classifier's estimate of the probability the stock is higher in 3 days, based on recent price patterns. This is the one number the AI actually reasons with instead of guessing.

Strike price

The fixed price written into the option contract — the price you can buy (call) or sell (put) at, regardless of where the stock actually is.

In the money / out of the money

A call is "in the money" if the stock price is above the strike (it's worth exercising). A put is "in the money" if the stock price is below the strike. "Out of the money" is the opposite — worthless if expiration happened right now.

Premium

The price of the option itself — what a buyer pays and a seller receives. Quoted per share; multiply by 100 for the actual dollar cost of one contract.

Kelly criterion

Not an options term — a general betting-math formula for how much of your bankroll to risk given your edge and payoff ratio. The system uses a conservative quarter of the full Kelly number, so even a good edge doesn't turn into an oversized bet.

Paper trading

A real brokerage account (Alpaca, here) that uses simulated money but real, live market prices. Every price, fill, and market condition is genuine — only the money is fake. This is legally required for a hackathon like this; nobody's real money is ever at risk.
